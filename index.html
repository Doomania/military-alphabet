History
Samuel Morse developed Morse code in 1837 for the electric telegraph. International standard adopted 1865.

· dot = 1 unit · — dash = 3 units · Letter gap = 3 units · Word gap = 7 units

SOS (· · · — — — · · ·) adopted as international distress signal in 1906. Chosen for simplicity, not as an abbreviation. Still valid on maritime and aviation frequencies today.

Quick Reference
NATO Time Zone Letters
How to Read Military Time
Format: 4-digit 24-hour (HHMM). No AM/PM, no colon. Midnight = 0000. Noon = 1200.

Pronunciation: "0830" = "Zero Eight Thirty Hours" · "1545" = "Fifteen Forty-Five Hours" · "0000" = "Zero Hundred Hours"

Why no AM/PM? Ambiguity kills. "3:00" in combat — morning or afternoon? 0300 vs 1500 is unambiguous.

11-Codes (California / Common)
Notes
10-codes were developed by APCO (Association of Public-Safety Communications Officials) in 1937 to shorten radio transmissions. Not fully standardized — codes vary by agency and country. After 9/11, FEMA recommended plain language over 10-codes for inter-agency clarity, but many departments still use them.

New Zealand Police use a modified set. Common NZ codes: 10-1 Signal weak · 10-4 Acknowledged · 10-20 Location · 10-76 En route.

Military Acronyms
Call Sign Structure
Format: "[Recipient], this is [Sender], [message], Over."

Call signs rotate and obscure identity from intercepts. "Actual" = the real commanding officer. "Maverick Actual" = Maverick unit's CO, not a subordinate.

Critical Rules
NEVER say "REPEAT" on radio. It's an artillery command meaning "fire again." Always say SAY AGAIN.

NEVER say WILCO + ROGER together. WILCO already implies Roger.

Why These Words?
ICAO tested each codeword against noise, interference, and 31 language groups. Alfa — prevents Spanish/Portuguese mispronunciation. Juliett (double-T) — forces French speakers to pronounce final consonant. Niner — avoids confusion with German "nein" (no).
